Types of Therapy Explained: Which Approach Is Right for You?

Illinois Valley Counseling Services · 8 min read

If you've ever looked into counseling, you've probably seen a wall of acronyms , CBT, DBT, EMDR, ACT , and wondered what any of it means. You're not alone. Most people don't know which type of therapy they need, and honestly, you don't have to.

Your therapist will figure out the right approach based on what you're dealing with. But knowing the basics can help you feel more confident walking in. Here's a plain-English guide to the types of therapy we offer at Illinois Valley Counseling Services , what each one is, how it works, and who it tends to help the most.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

What is CBT?

Good for: anxiety, depression, panic, phobias, negative thinking

CBT is one of the most researched and widely used forms of therapy. The core idea is simple: your thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are all connected , and changing one can change the others. You'll work with your therapist to identify unhelpful thought patterns (like "I always mess things up" or "nobody likes me"), challenge them, and replace them with more realistic, balanced thinking. it's practical, structured, and focused on giving you tools you can use right away.

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

What is DBT?

Good for: intense emotions, relationship struggles, self-harm, borderline personality

DBT was originally developed to help people who experience emotions very intensely. It teaches four key skills: mindfulness (staying present), distress tolerance (getting through a crisis without making it worse), emotion regulation (understanding and managing feelings), and interpersonal effectiveness (communicating your needs clearly). it's especially helpful if you feel like your emotions control you rather than the other way around.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

What is ACT?

Good for: chronic pain, anxiety, depression, life transitions, stress

ACT takes a different approach than CBT. Instead of trying to change your thoughts, ACT teaches you to accept them , to stop fighting with your own mind , and commit to actions that line up with your values. The goal isn't to feel better; it's to live a meaningful life even when difficult thoughts and feelings show up. it's particularly effective for people who feel stuck in a cycle of avoiding discomfort.

Play Therapy

What is Play Therapy?

Good for: children ages 3–12, behavioral issues, trauma, anxiety, grief

Children don't always have the words to explain what they're feeling , but they do have play. In play therapy, the therapist uses toys, games, art, and storytelling to help kids express emotions, process difficult experiences, and learn new coping skills. it's not just "playing" , it's a structured, evidence-based way for children to do the same kind of emotional work adults do in talk therapy, in a language they understand.

Family Systems Therapy

What is Family Systems Therapy?

Good for: family conflict, parenting challenges, blended families, communication problems

Family systems therapy looks at individuals in the context of their family , because nobody exists in a vacuum. The patterns, roles, and dynamics within a family shape how each person thinks and behaves. This approach helps families understand those patterns, break unhealthy cycles, and build healthier ways of relating to each other.

Solution-Focused Therapy

What is Solution-Focused Therapy?

Good for: specific problems, life transitions, goal-setting, short-term counseling

Rather than digging deep into the past, solution-focused therapy zeroes in on what you want your future to look like , and what's already working. Your therapist helps you identify your strengths, clarify your goals, and build on small successes. it's practical, forward-looking, and typically shorter-term than other approaches.

Trauma-Focused Therapy

What is Trauma-Focused Therapy?

Good for: PTSD, trauma, abuse, violence, accidents, loss

Trauma-focused therapy includes approaches specifically designed to help people heal from traumatic experiences. This includes Prolonged Exposure Therapy (gradually and safely facing trauma-related memories and situations you've been avoiding) and Cognitive Processing Therapy (working through beliefs about yourself and the world that trauma created). These are structured, evidence-based treatments that have been proven effective for PTSD.

Interpersonal Psychotherapy

What is Interpersonal Psychotherapy?

Good for: depression, grief, life transitions, relationship difficulties

Interpersonal psychotherapy focuses on your relationships and social roles , because when those areas of life are struggling, your mood often follows. It helps you navigate major life changes, resolve conflicts, process grief, and improve communication skills. The idea is that strengthening your connections with others strengthens your mental health.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)

What is EMDR?

Good for: PTSD, trauma, disturbing memories, anxiety related to past events

EMDR is a structured therapy that helps the brain reprocess traumatic memories so they lose their emotional charge. During a session, you'll briefly focus on a traumatic memory while your therapist guides your eye movements back and forth (or uses other bilateral stimulation like tapping). This helps your brain do what it does naturally during REM sleep , process and file away experiences , but for memories that got "stuck." It can feel unusual at first, but EMDR has strong research backing for its effectiveness with trauma.

How Your Therapist Chooses the Right Approach

Here's the honest truth: most therapists don't use just one method. They pull from several, depending on what you need. Your first few sessions are as much about your therapist getting to know you as they are about you getting to know them. They'll listen to your story, understand your goals, and tailor the approach accordingly.

You don't need to walk in and say "I want EMDR" or "I think I need CBT." You just need to show up. Our therapists at Illinois Valley Counseling Services are trained in multiple evidence-based approaches and will recommend what's most likely to work for you.

And if you try one approach and it doesn't click? That's okay. Therapy is a collaboration. You can always talk to your therapist about adjusting the direction.
